# Design 2: Chinese visual direction and initial Minchiate audit

Audience requirement recorded 2026-09-29: design for Chinese viewers who know
the visual traditions being referenced. Attractive approximation is not enough.
This is a research and revision brief, not a certification of historical accuracy
or a record of completed artwork revisions.

## Working direction

Use identifiable Chinese references for depicted material culture and living
things. Start with Ming court art and material culture as a proposed primary
reference, consistent with the existing court direction. Identify any deliberate
borrowing from another period or region rather than treating Tang, Ming, Qing,
and contemporary fantasy costume as interchangeable. The palette and illustration
technique may remain contemporary; structural details need evidence.

The existing 97-card inventory is Minchiate. Whether its European allegories
should also be reinterpreted through Chinese mythology is an open user choice.
Until resolved, do not silently replace the Western zodiac, Christian Faith,
centaur Cavaliers, or winged Fame with supposedly equivalent Chinese subjects.
They are distinct traditions. A locally occurring species and a culturally
recognizable depiction are also different questions.

## Initial findings

These are observations of the current PNGs and prompts, followed by proposed
changes. They are not claims that every Chinese viewer will respond alike.

| Priority | Cards/detail | Observation and revision direction |

| First | Leo | The current lion is naturalistic, with decorative cloth added. Its appearance alone does not establish an African subspecies. For a stronger Chinese visual reference, develop a lion from a specific Chinese sculptural or painted example: characteristic head, mane, muzzle, paws and posture. A Ming guardian lion is a promising reference; do not conflate guardian lions, lion-dance costumes, qilin and all creatures called suanni. Keep Leo recognizable as a lion. |
| First | Air | The large bird has a pale head and tail and a spread-wing silhouette that reads as a generic heraldic/bald-eagle-like design. The other birds are also nonspecific. Use a documented Chinese eagle/hawk painting and identifiable smaller birds, with coherent plumage and anatomy. Eagles themselves are not foreign to Chinese art. Do not substitute a fenghuang merely as a generic Chinese-looking bird. |
| First | House of the Devil; World; Trumpets | The burning stone tower and miniature towns read as European settings in these illustrations. Rework their buildings from a specific Chinese architectural reference, preserving the card's narrative. Distinguish ordinary houses, city gates, palaces and religious buildings; adding upturned eaves to the existing tower is insufficient. |
| Review | Water | The battened sails are a plausible Chinese maritime reference. The gilded phoenix figurehead, jeweled hull and pavilion-like stern are ornamental inventions unless supported by a chosen vessel reference. ## Reference notes

- **Lions:** the National Palace Museum explains Chinese lion imagery as a
  combination of accounts, observation and imagination. It is misleading to say
  that Chinese lions are simply an invented animal. Its
  [Ming lion painting discussion](https://south.npm.gov.tw/SelectionsDetailC004100.aspx?Cond=d57c0d65-bed6-40c5-ba13-06becc967fe6)
  describes tribute lions and their representation. The Met's
  [Ming seated lion, 1979.457.1](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/61824)
  supplies a specifically dated sculptural reference, rather than a generic
  modern souvenir image.
- **Birds:** Shanghai Museum identifies
  [Lin Liang's Two Eagles](https://www.shanghaimuseum.net/mu/frontend/pg/m/article/id/CI00020486)
  as Ming court painting on silk. The Met's
  [Bada Shanren Two Eagles](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/39540)
  discusses the earlier Ming eagle/hawk tradition and its associations with
  strength and courage. These are art references, not species identifications.
  The [CMS China raptor listing](https://raptors.cms.int/country/china) includes
  the golden eagle; changing the species solely because it occurs elsewhere
  would not address the visual problem.
- **Ships:** Burke Museum's
  [Chinese junk models](https://www.burkemuseum.org/news/model-chinese-junks-sail)
  explain battens and distinguish vessels from southern coastal China and
  riverboats. The Science Museum's
  [Shantung trading-junk model](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co41902/)
  provides a regional construction reference, but the model dates to 1931–1933.
  Neither source justifies calling the present fantasy vessel a Ming reconstruction.
- **Headdresses:** the Palace Museum's
  [Ming dragon-and-phoenix crown](https://www.dpm.org.cn/collection/embroider/231038.html)
  documents construction, material, provenance and ceremonial use. The National
  Museum of China's
  [Empress Xiaoduan crown](https://www.chnmuseum.cn/zp/zpml/kgfjp/202111/t20211126_252409.shtml)
  offers another documented object. These should inform distinct headdresses,
  not justify putting the same crown on every female figure.

## Revision workflow

For each revised component, record the specific reference, what it supports,
what remains an intentional adaptation, and the exact generation/edit prompt.
Keep original masters and prior card exports. Review source anatomy and cultural
details before fitting the result into the established card geometry; then rerun
the existing center, border, containment, dimensions and inventory checks.

Chinese-language museum descriptions should inform the brief. Feedback from
Chinese viewers familiar with the chosen tradition is valuable for judgments
that object records and automated checks cannot settle. Do not describe the
current 97 cards as culturally validated merely because their layout is complete.
